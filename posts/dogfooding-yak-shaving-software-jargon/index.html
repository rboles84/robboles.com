Dogfooding, Yak Shaving, and Why Software Jargon Is Secretly Useful

Dogfooding, yak shaving, RAG, grounding, drift, and blast radius sound ridiculous. Each one names a useful habit worth keeping.

Sep 12, 20268 min readLearning Lab
AI VocabularySoftware CultureSystems Thinking

● Last verified September 12, 2026 · no tools required

The jargon is absurd. The habits underneath it are useful.

If you're going to work with AI, it's important to take care of yourself.

Start your morning with some dogfooding. Get your chunking in early. Stay grounded. Remember to rehydrate. If you start hallucinating, check for drift. A little rubber ducking can help with stress. And whatever you do, try to keep your blast radius under control.

Follow this simple wellness routine and your RAG should be absolutely shredded by summer.

Unfortunately, most of those are established AI or software terms. I'm borrowing rehydration from software state management for the working context an AI session needs.

I've worked in software for years, so I'm probably desensitized to some of this. But AI has introduced a whole new group of people to an industry that has apparently been letting sleep-deprived engineers name things unsupervised for decades.

So, for anyone trying to follow the conversation, here's a completely normal day in AI and software.

7:30 AM — Start with some dogfooding

No dogs are harmed during dogfooding.

Probably.

Dogfooding basically means using your own product.

If I build something and tell everyone it's fantastic but refuse to use it myself, that's probably useful information.

The term sounds ridiculous, but the principle is great: don't just test your product in the nice little environment you created for it. Actually live with it.

Use it.

Get annoyed by it.

Discover that the button you thought was perfectly positioned is somehow the most irritating button ever created after you've clicked it 400 times.

That's dogfooding.

8:30 AM — Time for some rubber ducking

Now that we've fed the dog, get the duck.

Rubber duck debugging is exactly as sophisticated as it sounds.

You explain your problem to a rubber duck.

That's it.

The duck has no certifications. The duck did not attend your standup. The duck has never used Jira.

And yet somehow, halfway through explaining the problem, you realize:

"Oh."

Forcing yourself to explain something clearly often exposes what you weren't thinking clearly about.

AI has essentially given us a rubber duck that talks back.

Which is incredibly useful.

It also means the duck can now confidently tell you something completely wrong, so progress is complicated.

9:45 AM — Shave a yak

You came in this morning wanting to change one small thing.

Easy.

Except changing the thing requires updating a dependency.

The dependency won't build because your tooling is outdated.

You update the tooling.

That breaks something else.

You investigate that.

Now you're reading a GitHub issue from 2022 written by someone whose username looks like a Wi-Fi password.

It's almost lunch.

The thing remains unchanged.

You've been yak shaving.

Yak shaving is when completing one task requires another task, which requires another task, which requires another task, until you're seven layers removed from the thing you originally wanted to accomplish.

AI is remarkably good at helping with yak shaving.

AI is also remarkably good at creating exciting new yaks.

This is why occasionally asking, "What were we originally trying to do?" is an underrated engineering practice.

11:30 AM — Get your chunking in

We've dogfooded, rubber ducked, and shaved a yak.

Obviously, it's time to start chunking.

Chunking is considerably less violent than it sounds.

If you have a giant pile of information, you break it into smaller pieces that are easier to find and work with.

Think about a 900-page manual.

Instead of handing the AI all 900 pages every time you ask a question, you split the information into useful pieces so the system can find the parts that probably matter.

Those pieces are chunks.

You now have chunks.

Please don't put them in the dogfood.

12:15 PM — Work on your RAG

RAG stands for retrieval-augmented generation.

Which is exactly the sort of name you get when engineers are allowed to name things.

Plain English:

Find the useful stuff first. Then give that stuff to the AI.

You have 10,000 pages of information.

Your question probably needs 12 of them.

RAG is one way of finding those 12 before asking the AI to answer.

This is great.

There's just one tiny problem.

Having the correct answer somewhere in your giant pile of information does not mean the system actually retrieved it.

Your filing cabinet may contain your birth certificate.

That does not help if I confidently hand you a Chili's receipt instead.

RAG is useful.

RAG is not magic.

1:00 PM — Stay grounded

You've eaten lunch. You've worked on your RAG.

It's important to stay grounded.

Grounding an AI means tying its response to relevant, verifiable information instead of asking it to freestyle reality.

If I'm asking an AI about my project, I can give it the requirements, documentation, source material, current state, or other evidence it needs.

That's grounding.

Think less:

"Go to your room and think about what you've done."

Think more:

"Here are the actual documents. Please stop guessing."

2:00 PM — Hallucination time

And apparently we weren't grounded enough.

In AI, a hallucination is when the system produces erroneous or false information and presents it like everything is perfectly normal.

You ask:

"Where does the documentation say that?"

And the AI responds, metaphorically:

"Documentation?"

This gets simplified to "AI makes stuff up," which is useful enough for normal conversation.

But the interesting part is figuring out why you got the bad answer.

Maybe the model invented something.

Maybe your RAG retrieved the wrong information.

Maybe your source was outdated.

Maybe you didn't provide enough context.

Maybe the question was ambiguous.

Maybe you assumed something that wasn't true.

The useful lesson isn't "AI hallucinates, therefore AI bad."

It's this:

Confidence is not evidence.

This applies to humans too, unfortunately.

2:45 PM — Remember to rehydrate

You've had a long day.

Please rehydrate.

Not yourself.

The robot.

Even when a tool offers history or memory, a new AI session should not be assumed to carry perfect working knowledge of everything you've done across a long project.

Eventually you start a new session, change tools, lose useful context, or realize the current conversation has accumulated enough baggage to qualify for a checked bag.

For this kind of long AI-assisted project, I use rehydration as shorthand for:

Here's where we are. Here's what matters. Here's what we decided. Here's what's next. Please don't make me explain the last six weeks again.

I had to do exactly that in September 2026 when I reopened an article after two rounds of revision. The useful context wasn't in my head anymore. It was in the draft, the decision notes, and the checks we'd already run. Feeding those back into the next session turned a cold start into a continuation.

The goal isn't to make AI remember everything.

The goal is to preserve the important things somewhere reliable so you can give them back when they're needed.

It's project Gatorade.

3:30 PM — Check for drift

No cars are involved.

Here, I mean the project kind of drift: the work gradually moves away from what you originally intended.

Machine learning also uses drift more narrowly for changes in input data or prediction patterns. That's a different yak.

The annoying part is that every individual step can look reasonable.

Change this.

Improve that.

Clean this up.

Add this useful thing.

Tweak that behavior.

Three weeks later you look at the project and think:

"What happened to my website?"

That's drift.

It's one of the reasons having a clear definition of what you're actually building matters.

You need something to drift from.

4:15 PM — Reduce the blast radius

We started this morning feeding dogs and talking to ducks.

We are ending the afternoon discussing explosions.

Naturally.

Blast radius means:

If this goes wrong, how much else can it break?

Change one isolated piece of copy?

Small blast radius.

Give an autonomous agent permission to rewrite your production database, deploy directly to production, email every customer, and update your DNS records?

Larger blast radius.

Maybe don't do that one on Friday afternoon.

The concept matters more as AI systems become capable of actually doing things instead of merely suggesting things.

Giving AI more capability is useful.

Giving it unlimited capability because you're tired of clicking "approve" is how future blog posts get written.

5:00 PM — Congratulations, you're fluent

So tomorrow morning we'll dogfood the new workflow, rubber duck whatever breaks, try not to shave too many yaks, chunk our source material, work on our RAG, keep the AI grounded, watch for hallucinations, rehydrate when the context gets messy, check for drift, and keep the blast radius small.

Which is, somehow, now a sentence you understand.

I'm sorry.

Underneath all this completely unhinged terminology are some genuinely useful ideas. Use the things you build. Explain problems clearly. Notice when one task has turned into seventeen. Break large problems into manageable pieces. Find relevant information before making decisions. Ground conclusions in evidence. Don't mistake confidence for correctness. Preserve important context. Check that you're still solving the original problem. And when something can go wrong, think about how much damage it can do.

The portable rule is simple: when the jargon gets weird, translate it back into the action. If you can explain what the term asks you to do, you understand it. If you can't, another acronym won't save you.

Apparently, we could have just said all of that.

But then we'd have to find something else to do with the yak.

The completely normal flashcard deck

// take this with you

Read the term, say the plain-English action, then open the card to check yourself. Try not to cheat. The duck is watching.

Dogfoodingshow action

Use your own product.

Rubber duckingshow action

Explain the problem clearly enough to hear the gap.

Yak shavingshow action

Check whether the prerequisite work has swallowed the original task.

Chunkingshow action

Break a large body of information into useful pieces.

RAGshow action

Find the relevant material before asking the AI to answer.

Groundingshow action

Give the AI evidence and context instead of asking it to guess.

Hallucinationshow action

Treat confident output as a claim that still needs evidence.

Rehydrationshow action

Restore the decisions and context a new session needs.

Driftshow action

Compare the current work with the original intent.

Blast radiusshow action

Limit how much a mistake can affect.
