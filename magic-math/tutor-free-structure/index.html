Some players remove tutors because they want fewer repeated finishes. They want the deck to take different paths, to feel less scripted, or to leave room for a combo to emerge instead of being fetched on schedule.

Other players make almost the opposite argument. A tutor is a visible event. It exposes intent, gives the table a recognizable moment to reassess the game, and might be serving a toolbox rather than the same win every time. An untutored combo can arrive without that search step. In the player discussions that motivated this analysis, both positions appeared. Those discussions were qualitative—not a poll, and not evidence that either view is dominant.

That disagreement led me to a narrower question: after package-level tutor evidence disappears, what structural support is still visible in the combo itself?

Commander Spellbook is well suited to the package half of that question. It documents exact combo pieces, prerequisites, results, relationships, and commander requirements. It cannot observe complete decklists, draw probability, actual game repetition, player awareness, or table response. So this is not an article about how often combos happen. It is an article about what remains connected after one particular connection—searching for the pieces—is removed from the documented package.

Chapter 1 · What tutor-free means

What “tutor-free candidate” means

I analyzed 100,196 documented Commander Spellbook variants. After joining the exact cards to Oracle data and checking Oracle text, execution steps, and prerequisites for search evidence, 86,448 variants were classified as tutor-free candidates.

That label is narrower than it sounds. It means the documented package contains no positive evidence that a required component or a documented step searches for the pieces needed for that package. It does not mean the full deck contains no tutors. It does not mean the combo is hard to assemble, fair, casual, random, or unexpected.

All 86,448 tutor-free candidates passed the enrichment and review gates used here: high-confidence classification, exact-Oracle-ID coverage, no unmatched required component, and no manual-review flag. I am comfortable with the package-level label. I am not extending it to the deck around the package.

Chapter 2 · Structural support

The wrong binary

“Tutored or untutored” sounds like a complete description of combo consistency. It answers one real question: does the documented package search for required pieces? It does not answer every other way those pieces might be connected.

For this article, I use structural support to mean four observable package properties:

  • a component reused across many documented variants;
  • membership in a source-grouped candidate family;
  • survival of a strict fixed-core, one-varying-slot test;
  • a required commander.

These are not alternate forms of tutoring. A commander in the command zone is not a tutor. A card with hundreds of documented combo partners is not a tutor. A card that can occupy one structural slot is not a tutor. Each one simply tells us that the package is connected to something beyond its own row in the database.

“Support” is neutral here. It does not mean stronger, healthier, or more likely to appear in a game. Commander players often use “consistency” to bundle search, redundancy, card draw, commander access, mana reliability, and recovery into one word. Commander Spellbook can separate some of those package structures. It cannot turn them into a real-game consistency estimate.

Each row counts packages with exactly that combination. For example, the Widely reused piece + large related-combo group row (24,867) excludes packages that also require a commander or match the one-slot variation pattern. The article's “24,944 candidates have both H and F” is the broader figure — it also includes those additional cases. The two numbers measure different things; neither is wrong.

Among the 86,448 tutor-free candidates, 57,597 have the high-gravity signal, 29,944 have the large-family signal, 1,560 survive the strict one-slot test, and 858 require a commander. The categories overlap. Adding those counts would count many packages twice.

The largest overlap is between high gravity and large-family membership: 24,944 tutor-free candidates have both. But neither signal swallows the other. Some packages connect through a widely reused card without joining a large family. Others join a large family without containing a top-gravity component.

That overlap is why the layered view works better than a replacement label. If I called every family member “redundant,” I would overstate the relationship evidence. If I called every high-gravity package “consistent,” I would turn database participation into draw probability. Keeping the layers separate lets each one say exactly what it measures—and makes the gaps between them visible.

The cleanest summary is not “structured” versus “unstructured.” Of the tutor-free candidates, 22,784 have none of these four selected signals, 37,474 have one, 26,085 have two, and 105 have three. None have all four. That mix is the result: tutor-free packages are structurally different from one another.

“None of these four” still does not mean “no structure.” The rule is only checking these four measures. Under a deliberately strict isolation rule—no candidate-family membership and no component appearing in more than two variants—only ten tutor-free candidates qualified. Leyline of the Guildpact plus Coalition Victory is one. It is useful precisely because it pushes against the article's thesis instead of decorating it.

Chapter 3 · Widely reused pieces

Widely reused pieces

Component gravity is the number of documented Commander Spellbook variants containing a card. It is not card power, Commander popularity, deck inclusion rate, draw probability, or game frequency.

Under the extraction's rank-based top-1% definition, 57,597 of 86,448 tutor-free candidates contain at least one top-gravity component: 66.6%. That was the first place my simple expectation failed. I expected removing package-level tutor evidence to leave a much sparser map. Instead, many tutor-free packages still touch cards that recur throughout Spellbook.

The distribution also has a long tail. The top 100 cards account for 30.9% of exact-component appearances, while 1,367 exact cards appear in only one documented variant (18.8% of the 7,281 exact cards). A relatively small group participates in a large portion of documented combo structures. Many other cards appear once.

Sensei's Divining Top, Foundry Inspector, and Mystic Forge show the connected side: the package combines a top-gravity component with a large source family. Gravecrawler plus Phyrexian Altar contains a top-gravity component too, but the documented line still needs another Zombie. High gravity does not erase setup.

The contrast can exist inside one package. Nim Deathmantle, Ashnod's Altar, and Knight of the Last Breath pair a Spellbook-narrow component with a broad hub. Calling the whole package “common” would flatten the part that makes it specific.

Chapter 4 · Related combo groups

A family is not automatically redundancy

Within the tutor-free cohort, 29,944 of 86,448 candidates belong to a candidate family containing at least 100 variants: 34.6%. That sounds like a huge redundancy story. It sounded convincing until I asked what a candidate family actually guarantees.

The answer is: related structure, not interchangeable cards. A family may represent a shared engine, related packages, a result-sharing cluster, or many variants enumerated around one documented source group. Those are real relationships. They do not prove that the varying cards fill the same role in a deck.

The 100-variant cutoff is also an editorial threshold, not a natural boundary. At a minimum family size of 2, 64,081 tutor-free candidates qualify. The count falls to 59,576 at 10, 47,903 at 25, 38,338 at 50, 29,944 at 100, 16,476 at 250, and 5,550 at 500. The curve matters more than any single cutoff.

Staff of Domination plus Priest of Titania belongs to a large candidate family, but the line still asks for an established Elf board and a Priest that can tap. An Underworld Breach package with Malevolent Rumble, Mass Hysteria, and A Realm Reborn also sits in a large source family and needs cards already in the graveyard. Family size maps a neighborhood. It does not turn that neighborhood into a stack of clean replacements.

Enumeration matters here. One fixed engine documented with many possible payoffs or enablers can create many variant rows. Those rows are valid packages, but they are not independent observations of different decks or games. The family analysis describes the recorded surface of an engine, not how often players build or encounter it.

Chapter 5 · One-slot patterns

The stricter test changed the picture

To test the stronger redundancy claim, I used a narrower rule. The reproduced fixed core had to match the documented fixed core. Every qualifying package had to differ by exactly one exact component slot while preserving exact-card count, color identity, and normalized result-family structure.

Only 1,888 variants survived that strict one-slot structural test. 1,560 of them were tutor-free candidates. Broad connectivity is common in the database. Clean one-slot patterns are much narrower.

The two-card ladder (a definition, not the one true meaning)

Two definitions of a “two-card combo,” neither treated as the one true meaning:

  • 805 tutor-free candidates meet the clean-pair rule.
  • 306 of those also meet the conservative terminal-result rule.

Of 86,448 tutor-free candidates. Do not turn either value into the one true definition of a two-card combo.

This is where I need to be careful. Even the strict survivors are structural candidates, not proof that two cards have equal mana costs, timing, resilience, or deckbuilding roles. The test is stronger evidence of a substitution pattern than broad family membership. It does not capture every kind of real deck redundancy.

Aetherflux Reservoir plus Exquisite Blood is one strict example, and it still carries a substantial life-total prerequisite. Tidespout Tyrant plus Sol Ring is another, and it still needs another suitable nontoken permanent. Replacing one slot does not replace the rest of the game state.

Chapter 6 · Commander-required combos

The command zone is a different kind of support

Commander anchoring is the smallest selected layer: 858 tutor-free candidates require a commander according to the source field. That does not mean only 858 Spellbook combos involve legendary creatures or cards commonly used as commanders. It means those 858 tutor-free records explicitly mark a commander as required.

Krenko, Mob Boss with Skirk Prospector and Goblin Warchief shows both sides of the field. Krenko is a known, visible required piece. The documented package also needs a Goblin count tied to commander tax. The command zone changes where one piece begins. It does not find the other pieces or satisfy the board.

The field is narrower than the way players casually say, “My commander is part of the combo.” A commander might be an exact component, a repeatable enabler, or the deck's organizing engine. This analysis supports the first statement when the source marks that commander as required. It does not fully classify every role a commander can play.

Chapter 8 · What this answers

What this answers — and what it does not

Does removing tutors remove all structural support?

No. Many tutor-free candidates remain connected through reused components, related families, strict one-slot patterns, or commander requirements. But the zero-signal and isolated packages keep that answer bounded.

Does the remaining structure prove deck consistency?

No. That would require complete decklists, card counts, draw and selection effects, recursion, mana development, mulligans, and gameplay data. None of those probabilities are in Commander Spellbook.

Is redundancy basically another tutor?

No. A tutor searches a hidden zone and turns a choice into a specific card. Redundancy offers multiple cards or packages that might satisfy a role. Both can affect access, but they create different choices, reveal different information, and consume deck slots differently. Candidate-family membership alone does not establish redundancy.

Can an untutored combo still be predictable?

The structural analysis gives us clues: command-zone visibility, widely reused pieces, and broad engine connectivity. It cannot establish real gameplay predictability, recognition, surprise, or table response.

Does tutor-free mean more varied games?

Commander Spellbook cannot tell us. The player discussions include people who cut tutors to seek variety and people who replace search with redundant effects. Answering what happens in games requires complete decks and gameplay evidence.

Chapter 9 · Evidence and methodology

The evidence boundary

Part of what makes this trustworthy is being explicit about its edges.

What the data supports

  • 100,196 documented variants; 86,448 classified tutor-free at the package level.
  • Four separate, overlapping structural signals — never combined into a score.
  • Component gravity as documented-corpus participation, printed with its denominator.
  • Candidate-family coverage across all seven thresholds, not one “natural” cutoff.
  • The strict one-slot survivors (1,888 all-class; 1,560 tutor-free), each denominator kept distinct.

What it does not claim

  • That tutor-free combos are fair, sneakier, or that removing tutors changes nothing.
  • That candidate families are interchangeable cards.
  • That high gravity identifies the best or most powerful Commander cards.
  • Any real-game assembly probability, consistency per game, surprise, or power level.
  • Anything about full decks: the dataset contains no decklists or gameplay logs.

Methodology in plain language

This research uses Commander Spellbook source data covering 100,196 documented variants. Exact cards were joined to an auditable Oracle enrichment before package-level tutor evidence was classified from Oracle text, execution text, and prerequisites. Component gravity counts how many documented variants contain a card. Candidate families preserve source relationships and do not automatically establish substitution. The strict one-slot test requires a matching fixed core, one varying exact component, and matched package structure under the approved rule. Unresolved evidence stays outside the tutor-free class. The dataset contains no complete decklists and estimates no draw or gameplay probability. Related variants can enumerate the same engine and are not fully independent observations.

Better questions than tutor count alone

Tutor count is still worth discussing. It tells us whether the documented package searches for its pieces. It simply does not tell us everything supporting those pieces. For an actual deck conversation, I would ask: does the commander provide one of the pieces? How many cards can fill each role? Are the pieces independently useful in several engines? Is this package the deck's main plan, a backup ending, or an incidental overlap? Does the deck repeatedly search for the same line? How many slots exist primarily to assemble or protect it? What visible moments let opponents recognize the plan? What happens when the package is disrupted?

Most of those are not quantities Commander Spellbook can answer alone. They are better questions because they separate search, replacement options, visibility, protection, and recovery instead of compressing them into one moral label. Tutor count tells us whether the documented package searches for its pieces. It does not tell us how much structure remains around those pieces.
